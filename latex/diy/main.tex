\begin{abstract}
Social bias in large language models is a well-documented harm, and many mitigations have been proposed, but each typically targets a single dataset, task, or bias dimension, teaching the model to \emph{suppress} a symptom rather than to \emph{reduce} bias as a behavior. LLMs are trained on human text and inherit human bias, yet decades of social-psychology research already show \emph{how} prejudice can be reduced in people through validated cognitive interventions. We bring that literature into model training and inference, teaching models the same bias-reduction strategies so that debiasing becomes a transferable skill rather than a benchmark-specific patch. We introduce \diy{} (DIY), which operationalizes five established prejudice-reducing interventions, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, through three complementary techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} (in-context demonstrations), \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} (instruction tuning), and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} (intervention-guided self-revision). Evaluated against a broad set of debiasing baselines on bias and reasoning benchmarks, \diy{} consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with gains that generalize across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions, showing that grounding mitigation in transferable cognitive behavior beats dataset-specific suppression. Our code and data are available here\footnote{\url{https://github.com/chahatraj/DebiasItYourself}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models are trained on human-produced text and inherit the social biases that text encodes. They treat names, demographic descriptors, occupations, roles, and institutional contexts as task evidence even when these cues are irrelevant to the correct answer, producing outputs that systematically advantage some groups over others \citep{bolukbasi2016man, caliskan2017semantics, blodgett2020language, sheng2019woman, parrish-etal-2022-bbq}. As LLMs are deployed in hiring, education, healthcare, and content moderation, these failures move from benchmark curiosities to real harms borne disproportionately by already-marginalized groups \citep{bender2021dangers, weidinger2022taxonomy}. Bias mitigation is therefore not optional; the open question is how to do it in a way that actually generalizes.

A large literature has answered that question through targeted interventions: debiased word embeddings \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender}, counterfactual data augmentation \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}, prompt-based steering \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}, fairness-aware fine-tuning and preference optimization \citep{liang2020towards, ouyang2022training}, decoding-time constraints \citep{schick2021self}, and benchmark-specific filters or classifiers. These methods reliably move metrics on the slice they were designed for, but each typically targets a single dataset, a single task format, or a single bias dimension, and the gains rarely transfer when the cue changes from an identity term to an occupation, from a coreference task to an ambiguous question, or from a likelihood benchmark to free-form generation. The deeper issue is the unit of supervision: existing methods teach a model to \emph{suppress} a particular symptom of bias rather than to \emph{reduce} bias as a behavior the model can carry across inputs.

We take a different starting point. LLMs inherit human bias because they learn from human text, but humans also have decades of social-psychology research on \emph{how} prejudice is reduced in people, in the form of cognitive interventions validated in controlled studies \citep{devine2012longterm, allport1954nature, galinsky2000perspective}. The prejudice habit-breaking intervention of \citet{devine2012longterm}, in particular, identifies concrete bias-reducing strategies, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, each of which specifies what evidence to attend to, what heuristic to avoid, and what task-consistent response to produce. Our central premise is that these strategies are not just descriptions of human behavior; they define a transferable schema that a language model can be \emph{taught to apply}. Mitigation should target this reusable behavior rather than one benchmark or one dimension at a time.

We operationalize this premise in \diy{} (DIY), a structured supervision framework that turns the five interventions above into a single shared protocol for training and inference. \diy{} constructs biased and debiased instance pairs, labels each debiased target with the intervention that explains the desired behavior, and exposes this signal through three complementary techniques that vary how the supervision reaches the model: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} provides intervention-grounded demonstrations in context, \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} converts the same supervision into instruction-tuning targets, and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} uses an intervention-guided second pass to repair the model's first response. The three techniques compose, so the same intervention inventory can be delivered through demonstration alone, training alone, revision alone, or any combination. We do not claim that language models hold implicit attitudes in the human psychological sense; we claim that the procedural structure of these interventions transfers to model-facing supervision.

We evaluate \diy{} on a broad set of bias benchmarks, against a wide range of debiasing baselines, and on reasoning benchmarks that test whether mitigation erodes task competence. Our experiments span multiple model families and scales to test whether the gains are tied to a particular backbone or whether they reflect a portable recipe. \diy{} consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with the most stable gains when supervised teaching is composed with intervention-guided revision, and the pattern holds across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions. This supports our central claim: grounding mitigation in transferable cognitive behavior beats dataset-specific suppression.

This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
    \item We reframe bias mitigation as teaching a transferable bias-reducing \emph{behavior}, rather than suppressing bias on a single dataset, task, or dimension, and ground this framing in established prejudice-reduction theory from social psychology.
    \item We introduce \diy{}, a structured supervision framework that operationalizes five validated cognitive interventions into a unified training and inference protocol, instantiated through three complementary techniques: \textsc{Show}, \textsc{Teach}, and \textsc{Revise}.
    \item We construct an intervention-labeled supervision pipeline that pairs biased instances with debiased targets across social categories, instance types, and benchmark formats, so that the same inventory drives demonstrations, instruction tuning, and self-revision.
    \item We provide a broad empirical study across multiple bias benchmarks, debiasing baselines, reasoning benchmarks, model families, and scales, showing that \diy{} lowers bias error without trading away task competence and that the gains generalize beyond any single benchmark slice.
\end{enumerate}
