\begin{abstract}
Bias has long been studied in social psychology and cognitive science, where decades of research have produced a body of validated interventions that reduce stereotypical thinking and prejudiced responses in humans. We propose \diy{} (DIY), a cognitively grounded framework that translates five such interventions into debiasing procedures for large language models and delivers them through three established paradigms, \textsc{Show} (in-context demonstrations), \textsc{Train} (instruction tuning), and \textsc{Revise} (guided self-revision). Across three model families, five bias benchmarks, eleven debiasing baselines, and three reasoning benchmarks, \textsc{Train+Revise} and \textsc{Revise} alone attain the top two average ranks, lead the bias--reasoning tradeoff (mean bias as low as $2\%$ at $90\%$ reasoning accuracy), and transfer to unseen bias dimensions (up to $21.2\%$ reduction). Our code and data are publicly available.\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/DebiasItYourself}}
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\emph{``Prejudice is a habit that can be broken''} \citep{devine2012longterm}. Bias is not eliminated by awareness or restraint, but by deliberate cognitive practices that change how social information is processed when it is encountered. Decades of research in social psychology and cognitive science have empirically validated such practices in humans, showing that they specify what to attend to in a situation that invites a stereotyped response, what assumption to override, and what alternative to produce \citep{allport1954nature, devine2012longterm, galinsky2000perspective}. They are taught to people, practiced over time, and assessed for sustained change in behavior.

Bias in large language models is a well-documented harm \citep{bolukbasi2016man, caliskan2017semantics, blodgett2020language, sheng2019woman, parrish-etal-2022-bbq}, and a growing body of work has sought to mitigate it through debiased embeddings \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender}, counterfactual augmentation \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}, prompt-based steering \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}, fairness-aware training \citep{liang2020towards, bai2022constitutional}, and decoding-time constraints \citep{schick2021self}. These efforts have made measurable progress, but they have largely developed in isolation from the decades of psychological research on how bias is reduced in humans. Translating empirically validated cognitive interventions into language-model behavior is not just a question of whether they work, but a chance to ground bias mitigation in established theory of bias reduction, surface which forms of cognitive practices are most effective in language models, and open a research direction in which methods of debiasing can be systematically derived rather than ad hoc designed.

We present \diy{} (\diylabel{}), a cognitively-grounded approach to debiasing large language models. \diylabel{} adapts the prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm}, which integrates five empirically validated cognitive practices for reducing stereotypical thinking: \emph{stereotype replacement}, \emph{counter-stereotypic imaging}, \emph{individuation}, \emph{perspective-taking} \citep{galinsky2000perspective}, and \emph{positive contact} \citep{allport1954nature}. We translate each strategy into an explicit step-wise procedure the model can apply, and we deliver these procedures to the model through three established paradigms: in-context demonstrations (\textsc{Show}), instruction tuning (\textsc{Train}), and intervention-guided self-revision (\textsc{Revise}). In doing so, \diylabel{} treats debiasing as a procedure the model itself can learn and apply rather than a benchmark-specific fix. The three paradigms are intentionally straightforward; our aim is to provide a faithful first step toward grounding LLM debiasing in established theory of social psychology and cognitive sciences.

We evaluate \diylabel{} across three model families and scales, five bias benchmarks, eleven existing debiasing baselines spanning prompt-based, training-based, and inference-based families, and three reasoning benchmarks. The two top-ranked methods across bias benchmarks are both \diylabel{} configurations: \textsc{Train-Revise} and self-revision \textsc{Revise} alone. They also lead the bias--reasoning tradeoff, achieving mean bias as low as $2\%$ at $90\%$ reasoning accuracy, and they transfer to unseen bias dimensions, reaching up to $21.2\%$ bias reduction. We make the following contributions:

%\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0pt,parsep=3pt,topsep=3pt,partopsep=0pt]
\item We propose a psychology-grounded framework for translating cognitive bias-reduction strategies into debiasing procedures for language models, mapping five strategies from social psychology onto three established learning paradigms (\textsc{Show}, \textsc{Train}, \textsc{Revise}).
\item We implement the five cognitive strategies and evaluate them against eleven existing debiasing methods on 5 bias and 3 reasoning benchmarks, across model families, scales, and bias dimensions. We release the accompanying intervention dataset to support future work.
\item We show that the proposed cognitive interventions reduce bias more effectively than the existing debiasing methods on average, transfer to unseen bias dimensions, and preserve general reasoning ability across models, suggesting that cognitively-grounded bias mitigation is a promising direction for future work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We organize related work along three directions: NLP debiasing methods, NLP studies that draw on psychology, and the social-psychology literature on bias reduction.

\paragraph{Bias mitigation in language models.} Existing work falls into four families. \emph{Representation-level} methods debias learned embeddings or sentence representations \citep{bolukbasi2016man, liang2020towards}. \emph{Data-centric} methods augment training with counterfactual or balanced examples \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}. \emph{Inference-time} methods prompt or self-correct toward fairer outputs. And \emph{alignment-based} methods incorporate human or model-derived preferences during training \citep{bai2022constitutional}. Across these families, methods are typically tuned to a particular benchmark or social dimension, and improvements often reflect changes to the model's outputs on the benchmark it was tuned for. Standard bias benchmarks themselves score the model's outputs: stereotype-pair preferences \citep{nangia-etal-2020-crows, nadeem-etal-2021-stereoset}, ambiguous QA \citep{parrish-etal-2022-bbq}, counterfactual coreference \citep{zhao-etal-2018-gender, rudinger-etal-2018-gender}, and bias in open-ended generation \citep{dhamala2021bold, nozza-etal-2021-honest, smith-etal-2022-im}.

\paragraph{Cognitive framings in NLP.} A smaller line of work borrows psychology-inspired strategies. Self-debiasing prompts a model to suppress its own biased generations \citep{schick2021self}, moral self-correction uses instruction-following to elicit fairer responses \citep{ganguli2023capacity}, \citet{raj2024breaking} apply the contact hypothesis to debias LLMs, and \citet{xu-etal-2024-walking} use perspective-taking prompts. Each of these works draws on a single strategy, and the connection to social-psychology research on prejudice reduction has remained largely thematic.

\paragraph{Bias reduction in human cognition.} Bias reduction in humans has been studied empirically for decades. \citet{allport1954nature} established that structured intergroup contact reduces prejudice, \citet{galinsky2000perspective} showed that perspective-taking reduces stereotype expression, and \citet{devine2012longterm} integrated these and related findings into a habit-breaking intervention based on five practices, with reductions in implicit race bias sustained over time. The consistent finding across this literature is that bias is reduced by changing how social information is processed at the moment of encounter, not by suppressing individual responses. Where prior cognitive framings in NLP each adopt a single strategy, \diylabel{} draws on five complementary practices grounded in \citet{devine2012longterm} and studies them under three established learning paradigms in language models, evaluating across benchmarks.

\section{The DIY Framework}
\label{sec:data-methods}

We describe the five cognitive bias-reduction strategies drawn from social psychology, and the dataset that instantiates them as biased--debiased pairs.


\subsection{Bias-Reducing Interventions}
\label{sec:strategies}

We draw on the prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm}, which integrates five cognitive practices that have been empirically validated in social psychology to reduce stereotypical thinking with sustained effects. Each practice targets a distinct mechanism through which stereotypes and biases arise in reasoning and is specified as an explicit step-wise procedure, which makes it directly applicable as model-facing input. The practices can be applied independently or in combination, and are agnostic to model architecture, task, or domain.

\paragraph{\srlabel{}.}
This practice disrupts biased reasoning by explicitly identifying a stereotype and substituting it with a fairer, individualized interpretation. It proceeds in three steps: (i) recognizing whether a stereotype or bias is being invoked, (ii) reflecting on why the stereotype may be inaccurate, overgeneralized, or harmful, and (iii) replacing the biased assumption with a neutral or evidence-based alternative. The goal is not to suppress reasoning, but to redirect it away from group-based generalizations.

\paragraph{\cilabel{}.}
This practice reduces bias by strengthening associations that contradict prevailing stereotypes. After identifying the stereotype being invoked, the model is prompted to imagine a real or hypothetical individual who defies the stereotype and to elaborate concrete details about this counter-stereotypic example. By reinforcing alternative associations, the strategy weakens the automatic activation of stereotypical beliefs during reasoning.

\paragraph{\indlabel{}.}
This practice shifts attention from social categories to individual-specific information. It instructs the model to consciously decenter group membership, gather relevant individuating details such as personal traits, behaviors, or context, and revise the initial judgment based on this information. The resulting behavior mitigates bias by replacing category-based inference with case-specific reasoning.

\paragraph{\ptlabel{}.}
This practice encourages the model to adopt the viewpoint of the person being stereotyped. It involves simulating the thoughts, feelings, or experiences of the targeted individual and integrating this perspective into the final response. By foregrounding lived experience, the strategy reframes biased assumptions and promotes more empathetic and context-aware reasoning.

\paragraph{\pclabel{}.}
This practice draws on contact theory, which suggests that meaningful positive interactions reduce prejudice. It prompts the model to imagine or recall a constructive interaction with a member of the stereotyped group, describe the interaction in detail, and generalize its implications to challenge the original stereotype. The emphasis is on affective engagement rather than factual correction alone.

Together, these five strategies form a modular cognitive intervention toolkit. Each strategy can be deployed independently, enabling fine-grained analysis of their individual effects, or combined to create stronger debiasing interventions. Crucially, all strategies operate at the level of reasoning and interpretation, rather than relying on task-specific labels or outcome supervision.

\subsection{DIY Dataset Creation}
\label{sec:data}

We construct a synthetic debiasing dataset using a self-instruct-style generation pipeline grounded in the five cognitive interventions above. The dataset pairs biased inputs with debiased outputs. Each biased input is realized in three textual forms: an \emph{opinion} (a direct biased statement, e.g., \emph{``All [identity] are dishonest''}), a biased \emph{action} (a sentence in which the opinion drives a discriminatory behavior), and a biased \emph{event} (a sentence describing an event that leads to forming the opinion). Each debiased output follows one of the five cognitive interventions. The resulting data supports both supervised instruction tuning and inference-time in-context debiasing.

\paragraph{Seed Identity Generation.}
We first generate a diverse set of demographic identity descriptors that serve as the subjects of potential bias. These include both single-identity configurations (e.g., \emph{``a Black man''}) and paired-identity configurations (e.g., \emph{``a Black man and a White man''}), enabling the construction of bias scenarios involving individual and comparative judgments. Identities are sampled across multiple demographic dimensions and are intentionally generated independently of any specific task or outcome.

\paragraph{Bias Concept Generation.}
Given a set of identity descriptors, we next generate bias concepts that reflect commonly observed stereotypical traits, assumptions, or associations (e.g., perceived competence, socioeconomic status, athletic ability). These concepts are generated without attaching ground-truth labels or correctness constraints, and are intended solely to elicit biased reasoning when combined with identity information. Bias concepts may target a single individual or implicitly contrast multiple identities.

\paragraph{Biased Instance Generation.}
Biased instances are created by combining identity descriptors and bias concepts within open-ended scenarios, such as professional, social, or everyday contexts. A language model is prompted to synthesize these components into coherent natural language inputs designed to plausibly trigger stereotypical reasoning. This step yields bias-prone prompts without explicitly instructing the model to behave unfairly, mirroring how bias arises in unconstrained language use.

\paragraph{Debiased Instance Generation.}
For each biased instance, we generate a corresponding debiased output using a specified cognitive intervention strategy. The model is prompted to apply the selected strategy by explicitly following its step-wise reasoning structure (e.g., recognizing the stereotype, reflecting on its limitations, and revising the interpretation). This results in aligned biased--debiased pairs that can be formatted as instruction--response examples for supervised fine-tuning or as demonstrations for in-context debiasing at inference time.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementations}

\diylabel{} brings the cognitive interventions of Section~\ref{sec:strategies} to a language model in three complementary ways, \showlabel{}, \trainlabel{}, and \reviselabel{}, which we describe below.

\paragraph{\showlabel{}.}
At inference, the chosen intervention and a small number of biased--debiased example pairs (worked through the intervention's three steps) are placed in the prompt as in-context demonstrations. The model conditions on the demonstrations and produces a debiased response under the same step structure, without parameter updates. We evaluate \showlabel{} per intervention, running each of the five interventions as a separate condition, and additionally report an aggregated \textsc{All-strategies} setting that mixes the five.

\paragraph{\trainlabel{}.}
The intervention-labeled biased--debiased pairs are converted into instruction-tuning targets and used to fine-tune the model with parameter-efficient adaptation. After training, the model can apply the intervention without explicit demonstrations or intervention text in the prompt.

\paragraph{\reviselabel{}.}
The model first produces an unconstrained response to the input. It is then prompted to apply a selected intervention to its own response by following the same three steps, and the revised response replaces the first. As with \showlabel{}, \reviselabel{} is evaluated per intervention and in the \textsc{All-strategies} aggregation. When \reviselabel{} is composed with \trainlabel{} (\diytrainreviselabel{}), the revision pass is run on both the fine-tuned and the base model, which allows us to separate the contribution of training from that of intervention-guided revision. When \showlabel{} is composed with \trainlabel{} (\diytrainshowlabel{}), the in-context demonstrations are drawn from the same intervention the model was trained on.

The three approaches are independent in how they reach the model and compatible in input. All three draw on the same intervention-labeled data of Section~\ref{sec:data}, and any combination of \showlabel{}, \trainlabel{}, and \reviselabel{} continues to operate over the same five interventions.

\paragraph{Evaluated configurations.}
We evaluate \diylabel{} across the cells of the $\textsc{Show}\times\textsc{Train}\times\textsc{Revise}$ space, naming each configuration by the approaches it activates. The single-approach configurations are \diytrainlabel{} (instruction tuning alone), \diyshowlabel{} (in-context demonstrations alone), and \diyreviselabel{} (guided self-revision alone). The two-approach configurations are \diytrainshowlabel{} (training combined with demonstrations) and \diytrainreviselabel{} (training combined with revision). The unmodified baseline is denoted \baselabel{}. For every configuration that includes a prompt component, we additionally run a 0-shot and a 1-shot variant, where the shot count is the number of in-context demonstrations of the selected cognitive intervention.

\paragraph{Training scale.}
For \trainlabel{}, we train one fine-tuned checkpoint per (intervention, instance-type) cell of the data, plus an \textsc{All-strategies} checkpoint that combines all five. Each checkpoint is trained on a 500-example subset (sampled with a fixed seed) for parity across cells. The same LoRA configuration (rank $256$, $\alpha=256$, learning rate $2{\times}10^{-4}$, three epochs) is applied to all three models, \llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct, \llama{}-3.1-70B-Instruct, and \qwen{}-QwQ-32B.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate \diy{} on \llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct, \llama{}-3.1-70B-Instruct, and \qwen{}-QwQ-32B, using five bias benchmarks: CrowS-Pairs \citep{nangia-etal-2020-crows}, StereoSet \citep{nadeem-etal-2021-stereoset}, BBQ \citep{parrish-etal-2022-bbq}, WinoBias \citep{zhao-etal-2018-gender}, and WinoGender \citep{rudinger-etal-2018-gender}; and three reasoning benchmarks: Balanced COPA \citep{kavumba-etal-2019-choosing}, ARC-Challenge, and ARC-Easy \citep{clark2018arc}. We compare against eleven prior debiasing methods spanning three families. \emph{Prompt-based} methods elicit fairer responses through prompt engineering: SelfDebias \citep{gallegos2024selfdebiasing} and RSB \citep{kamruzzaman2025rsb}. \emph{Training-based} methods modify model parameters: DebiasNLG \citep{wang-demberg-2024-parameter}, DebiasLLMs \citep{dong2024disclosure}, PEFT \citep{zhao-etal-2025-debiasing}, DPO \citep{dai2025mitigating}, LFTF \citep{qin2025lftf}, and BiasEdit \citep{xu-etal-2025-biasedit}. \emph{Inference-based} methods steer or correct outputs at decoding time: FairSteer \citep{li2025fairsteer}, CAL \citep{sun-etal-2024-causal}, and BBA \citep{lin2026bba}. Implementation details for \diy{} are provided in the appendix.

\subsection{Bias Mitigation}
\label{sec:rq1}

The five bias benchmarks use different native scoring conventions, so we report two normalized quantities that are commensurable across them. The \emph{bias score} on a benchmark is the absolute distance from the value an unbiased model would produce, $\mathrm{score} = |\mathrm{score}_{\text{benchmark}} - \mathrm{score}_{\text{unbiased}}|$, where $\mathrm{score}_{\text{unbiased}}$ equals $50$ for CrowS-Pairs and StereoSet and $0$ for BBQ, WinoBias, and WinoGender. The \emph{average rank} of a method ranks all methods on each benchmark by bias score (rank $1$ = lowest) and averages across benchmarks, $\mathrm{avg\,rank}(m) = \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{b \in \mathcal{B}} \mathrm{rank}_b(m)$, with $\mathcal{B}$ the set of bias benchmarks. Both quantities are lower-is-better.

\paragraph{Cross-benchmark performance.}
On every tested model, the lowest average rank across the five bias benchmarks is achieved by a \diylabel{} configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-rank}). \diytrainreviselabel{} ($3.83$) leads on \llama{}-3.1-8B, and \diyreviselabel{} leads on both \qwen{}-QwQ-32B ($3.50$) and \llama{}-3.1-70B ($4.83$). The lead is consistent across model families and scales: intervention-guided revision (\diyreviselabel{}, \diytrainreviselabel{}) occupies both top-two slots on every model. The strongest non-\diylabel{} baselines (DebiasLLMs, PEFT, BiasEdit, SelfDebias) trail the leading \diylabel{} configuration by an average rank of $1.5$ to $2.3$ on the smaller models, narrowing to $1.0$ on \llama{}-3.1-70B.

\end{insightbox}

\paragraph{Method Comparison.}
Across the five \diylabel{} configurations, intervention-guided revision contributes the largest share of the bias reduction, and combining it with instruction tuning yields the strongest overall configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:debiasing-shot-llama8b}; \llama{}-3.1-70B and \qwen{}-QwQ-32B in Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:app-debiasing-shot-llama70b}--\ref{fig:app-debiasing-shot-qwen}). \diyreviselabel{} reduces bias substantially across all three models. For example, on \llama{}-3.1-8B CrowS-Pairs the bias score drops from $15.2$ at \baselabel{} to $2.45$, and composing \diyreviselabel{} with \diytrainlabel{} sharpens it further to $0.860$. \diytrainlabel{} alone produces a smaller and less consistent reduction, suggesting that parameter-level supervision is most useful when paired with an inference-time procedure rather than applied in isolation. \diyshowlabel{} shows a different pattern. Zero-shot demonstrations are competitive with the leading configurations, while adding more demonstrations does not consistently help. Shot effects are technique-dependent more broadly. Revision benefits modestly from additional demonstrations, while training-based configurations are largely insensitive to shot count.

\begin{insightbox}
\smash{\notebookpencil}~\textbf{Takeaway 2.} Intervention-guided revision is the strongest single technique, and pairing it with instruction tuning yields the most reliable configuration. Instruction tuning alone offers limited improvement; in-context demonstrations are most effective at zero-shot.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Reasoning Ability}
\label{sec:rq2}

\paragraph{Reasoning across DIY methods and baselines.}
Across all five \diylabel{} configurations and both shot settings, reasoning accuracy on Balanced COPA, ARC-Challenge, and ARC-Easy stays at or above the unmodified \baselabel{} on \qwen{}-QwQ-32B (Figure~\ref{fig:reasoning-preservation-qwen}; \llama{}-3.1-8B and \llama{}-3.1-70B in Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:app-reasoning-preservation-llama8b}--\ref{fig:app-reasoning-preservation-llama70b}). Several configurations exceed \baselabel{}; \diytrainshowlabel{} reaches $97.8\%$ on ARC-Easy against $88.5\%$ for \baselabel{}, and \diytrainlabel{} adds about seven points on ARC-Challenge. Revision-based, training-based, and demonstration-based configurations all stay within the same accuracy band, with no systematic degradation from any added supervision component. The only consistent dip across models is \diyshowlabel{} at $1$-shot on Balanced COPA, which falls a few points below \baselabel{} on \qwen{}-QwQ-32B and a larger margin on \llama{}-3.1-8B; this aligns with the \diyshowlabel{} $1$-shot bias-reduction instability noted earlier and is consistent with demonstration content driving the dip rather than the technique itself. Supervising the model on intervention-guided behavior therefore does not erode general reasoning, and on the larger model can incidentally improve it.

\paragraph{Bias--reasoning tradeoff.}
A central concern for any debiasing method is that bias reductions should not come at the cost of general task competence. \diylabel{} configurations sit on the favorable corner of the bias--reasoning frontier across all three models, achieving low mean bias without the reasoning collapse seen in several baselines (Figure~\ref{fig:bias-reasoning-pareto}). On \llama{}-3.1-8B, \diyreviselabel{} and \diytrainreviselabel{} reach the lowest mean bias of any method while preserving reasoning accuracy near $70\%$. DebiasLLMs reaches a similar bias level only by dropping reasoning below $50\%$. The same pattern holds on \qwen{}-QwQ-32B, where \diytrainreviselabel{} achieves both the highest reasoning accuracy ($\sim 93\%$) and a mean bias comparable to the strongest debiasing baselines, while BiasEdit and DebiasLLMs reach low bias only at substantially lower reasoning. One explanation is that methods that lower bias by inducing vague or refusal-style outputs lose the ability to answer task questions correctly, whereas \diylabel{} preserves it because the supervision target is intervention-guided behavior on bias-sensitive input rather than output suppression.

\end{insightbox}

\section{Conclusion}
Bias mitigation in language models can be approached as a problem already studied for decades in another field. The empirical study in this paper supports that view. Cognitive interventions developed and validated for human bias reduction transfer to LLM behavior, with guided self-revision producing the most consistent gains and general reasoning ability preserved across configurations. The intervention-labeled dataset we construct alongside the study is a starting point for further work grounded in social-psychology theory rather than benchmark-specific signals, and we expect more of the literature on prejudice reduction to translate into model-facing input than is examined here.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Synthetic intervention data.} Validated examples of cognitive bias-reduction strategies in social psychology are few and tied to narrow case studies, which is not enough to instruction-tune a language model. We address this by generating synthetic intervention-labeled biased--debiased pairs at scale. Generation-time artifacts in the prompting model may propagate into the data, and the synthetic distribution may not capture the full breadth of social-psychology intervention examples a larger human-curated dataset could provide.

\paragraph{Output behavior versus internal processing.} The evidence we observe is bias scores on benchmarks. We cannot directly verify whether the model's underlying processing of social cues has changed or whether the model has become better at producing benchmark-shaped output that scores well under our bias-score formulation. Reasoning preservation provides indirect evidence against pure output suppression, but a direct test of internal processing is outside the scope of this work.

\paragraph{Coverage of long-form generation and multi-hop reasoning.} Our bias evaluation uses likelihood, multiple-choice, and pair-ranking benchmarks, and our reasoning evaluation uses commonsense and grade-school science multiple-choice tasks. We do not evaluate \diylabel{} on long-form generation, dialogue, or multi-hop reasoning benchmarks, so claims about preserved task competence and bias reduction are bounded by these task formats.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

\paragraph{Use of bias-reduction interventions.} \diylabel{} adapts social-psychology interventions developed for human prejudice reduction into model-facing input. We make no claim that the model engages in bias reduction in the same cognitive sense as a human; we claim only that the procedural form of the interventions is a useful target for the model. Treating model behavior as analogous to human cognition risks overclaiming and is explicitly disclaimed.

\section{Data}
\label{app:data}

This appendix complements Section~\ref{sec:data} with full details of the dataset used by \trainlabel{} and \showlabel{}. We describe the generator stack and prompts, the demographic coverage, the size of each split, the filtering and quality-control passes, and the safety considerations for synthetic content involving social groups.

\paragraph{Generator stack.}
The dataset is produced by a four-stage pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Identity descriptors and bias concepts are generated by \llama{}-3.3-70B-Instruct at temperature $0.7$ for diversity in identities and at temperature $0.2$ for the bucketed concept stage where coverage matters more than novelty. Biased and debiased instances are generated by \llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct at temperature $0.7$. We use the same generator family (\llama{}) for the data construction and \llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct as the primary model under evaluation. Using a small model as the bias generator (rather than a larger or proprietary model) is intentional: it avoids leaking capabilities of a stronger generator into the supervision signal that the same model is then trained on.

\paragraph{Demographic coverage.}
Identity descriptors are seeded from the HolisticBias inventory \citep{smith-etal-2022-im} after merging two release versions and removing the \emph{preference} subtree. The seed contains identities organized along ten bias dimensions (\textsc{Race}/\textsc{Ethnicity}, \textsc{Gender Identity}, \textsc{Sexual Orientation}, \textsc{Religion}, \textsc{Nationality}, \textsc{Age}, \textsc{Disability}, \textsc{Body Type}, \textsc{Socioeconomic Status}, and \textsc{Political Ideology}). We generate both single-identity descriptors (e.g., \emph{``a Black man''}) and paired-identity descriptors (e.g., \emph{``a Black man and a White man''}) to support both individual and comparative judgments. The flattened identity inventory is deduplicated and stripped of placeholder strings before bias-concept generation.

\paragraph{Bias concepts.}
For each identity descriptor we generate stereotypical traits, assumptions, and associations. The body of the paper notes that these concepts are produced ``without attaching ground-truth labels or correctness constraints.'' We clarify the design rationale here. The role of a bias concept is not to encode a ground-truth claim about a group; it is to elicit the kind of spurious association a model may produce on its own when faced with a bias-sensitive input. The downstream supervision target is the \emph{debiased} response, not the bias concept. Bias concepts therefore function as triggers to generate biased instances; they do not enter the training signal as labels for the model to learn or reproduce.

\paragraph{Filtering and quality control.}
We apply four filtering passes during construction. (i) The HolisticBias seed is filtered to remove the \emph{preference} subtree and to drop placeholder entries. (ii) Generated identity descriptors are deduplicated by exact string match and length-bounded to remove malformed outputs. (iii) Bias concepts are bucketed by identity dimension and limited per dimension to prevent overrepresentation. (iv) Biased and debiased instances are checked against a regular-expression list to remove outputs that drop or rename the placeholder identity. We additionally hold out a portion of the data as a development split for spot-checking generation quality during pipeline iteration.

\paragraph{Dataset size and splits.}
The full pipeline yields a corpus of intervention-labeled biased--debiased pairs spanning all ten bias dimensions, all five cognitive practices, and three biased-instance forms (opinion, action, event). For \trainlabel{}, we sample $500$ instances per (intervention, instance-form) cell with a fixed seed, training one parameter-efficient fine-tune per cell and a combined \textsc{All-strategies} fine-tune. For \showlabel{}, we sample in-context demonstrations from the same pool. Evaluation never reuses training instances; bias evaluations use the held-out CrowS-Pairs, StereoSet, BBQ, WinoBias, and WinoGender benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-results}). Generalization experiments use a leave-one-dimension train/test protocol where supervision is restricted to one bias dimension and evaluation is on the others.

\paragraph{Safety and intended use.}
The dataset contains synthetic biased text by construction; it cannot be used as a corpus of human stereotypes about real groups, nor should it be used to characterize any actual group. Each biased instance is paired with a debiased counterpart under one of the five cognitive practices, and only the debiased outputs are training targets. Released artifacts include the data, the generation prompts, and the filtering scripts so that the pipeline can be inspected, audited, and extended. The data is released for research on bias mitigation in language models and not for use as a stereotype reference, a content classifier, or a generative source for biased text.

\section{Dataset Statistics}
\label{app:dataset-statistics}

The full intervention-labeled corpus contains biased--debiased pairs spanning ten bias dimensions, three biased-instance forms (opinion, action, event), and five cognitive-practice labels (\srlabel{}, \cilabel{}, \indlabel{}, \ptlabel{}, \pclabel{}). \section{Computational Setup and Hyperparameters}
\label{app:compute}

\paragraph{Models.}
\diylabel{} is evaluated on three open-weight instruction-tuned models: \llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct (8B parameters), \llama{}-3.1-70B-Instruct (70B parameters), and \qwen{}-QwQ-32B (32B parameters). \llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct is the primary model for the headline results; the larger \llama{}-3.1-70B-Instruct and the alternative-family \qwen{}-QwQ-32B test whether the pattern of findings transfers to higher-capacity and architecturally distinct backbones. \trainlabel{} is applied to all three models with identical LoRA hyperparameters (see below).

\paragraph{Fine-tuning.}
\trainlabel{} adapters are trained with LoRA on all three evaluated models (\llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct, \llama{}-3.1-70B-Instruct, and \qwen{}-QwQ-32B) using the same configuration: rank $r=256$, $\alpha=256$, learning rate $2{\times}10^{-4}$, three epochs, mixed-precision optimization. We did not run a hyperparameter search; values were fixed in advance and held constant across all models and all (intervention, instance-form) cells to keep results comparable.

\paragraph{Inference.}
For \showlabel{} and \reviselabel{} we use greedy decoding with the model's default generation settings. For \trainlabel{}, we evaluate the LoRA-adapted checkpoint with the same decoding settings as the base model.

\paragraph{Compute infrastructure.}
Fine-tuning and inference run on a single-node multi-GPU configuration with NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPUs. Total compute for the experiments reported in this paper, including fine-tuning runs, inference passes across all three models and all evaluation benchmarks, and synthetic data generation, is on the order of a few hundred GPU-hours. We did not perform any large-scale hyperparameter sweep.

\section{Strategy Examples}
\label{sec:strategy-examples}

\begin{strategybox}[Stereotype Replacement]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Recognize:} This statement relies on a gender stereotype that equates emotional expression with poor leadership ability.

\textbf{Reflect:} This generalization is inaccurate and overlooks evidence that emotional intelligence can enhance leadership effectiveness.

\textbf{Replace:} Leadership ability depends on skills, experience, and judgment, not gender.
\end{strategybox}

\begin{strategybox}[Counter-Stereotype Imaging]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Recognize:} This statement invokes a stereotype that questions women's suitability for leadership.

\textbf{Imagine:} Consider Jacinda Ardern, former Prime Minister of New Zealand.

\textbf{Reinforce:} She demonstrated calm, decisive leadership during major crises while also leading with empathy, contradicting the stereotype.
\end{strategybox}

\begin{strategybox}[Individuating]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Attend:} This statement applies a broad group-based judgment rather than evaluating an individual.

\textbf{Gather:} Angela Merkel served as Chancellor of Germany for 16 years and was widely recognized for her pragmatic and steady leadership.

\textbf{Adjust:} Evaluating leadership based on individual evidence contradicts the stereotype and supports fairer judgment.
\end{strategybox}

\begin{strategybox}[Perspective Taking]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Adopt:} Take the perspective of a woman in a leadership role facing recurring skepticism.

\textbf{Simulate:} She may find that assertiveness is labeled as emotional, while similar behavior in male peers is praised, creating unequal expectations.

\textbf{Integrate:} From this perspective, the stereotype reflects bias rather than leadership ability.
\end{strategybox}

\begin{strategybox}[Positive Contact]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Imagine:} Recall a positive professional interaction with a woman leading a high-pressure team.

\textbf{Engage:} She acknowledged team concerns while clearly guiding decisions, improving focus and morale.

\textbf{Extend:} This experience shows that emotional awareness can be a leadership strength, challenging the stereotype.
\end{strategybox}
