\begin{abstract}
Social bias in large language models is a well-documented harm, and many mitigations have been proposed, but each typically targets a single dataset, task, or bias dimension, teaching the model to \emph{suppress} a symptom rather than to \emph{reduce} bias as a behavior. LLMs are trained on human text and inherit human bias, yet decades of social-psychology research already show \emph{how} prejudice can be reduced in people through validated cognitive interventions. We bring that literature into model training and inference, teaching models the same bias-reduction strategies so that debiasing becomes a transferable skill rather than a benchmark-specific patch. We introduce \diy{} (DIY), which operationalizes five established prejudice-reducing interventions, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, through three complementary techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} (in-context demonstrations), \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} (instruction tuning), and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} (intervention-guided self-revision). Evaluated against a broad set of debiasing baselines on bias and reasoning benchmarks, \diy{} consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with gains that generalize across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions, showing that grounding mitigation in transferable cognitive behavior beats dataset-specific suppression. Our code and data are available here\footnote{\url{https://github.com/chahatraj/DebiasItYourself}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

After a decade of debiasing research, language models are still biased. Mitigation methods lower stereotype scores on the benchmarks they target, but the same models continue to produce stereotyped outputs on related tasks, on related social dimensions, or on small reformulations of the original benchmark \citep{bolukbasi2016man, caliskan2017semantics, blodgett2020language, sheng2019woman, parrish-etal-2022-bbq, bender2021dangers, weidinger2022taxonomy}. The field has learned how to lower bias metrics without changing the underlying behavior of the model.

Why? Existing methods, including debiased embeddings \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender}, counterfactual augmentation \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}, prompt-based steering \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}, fairness-aware fine-tuning and preference optimization \citep{liang2020towards, ouyang2022training}, and decoding-time constraints \citep{schick2021self}, share three problems:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Narrow target.} Each method optimizes a metric on one dataset, one task format, or one bias dimension, and leaves the rest of the model untouched.
    \item \textbf{Suppression, not reduction.} Models are pushed to avoid bias-coded outputs, often by becoming vague, evasive, or refusing to answer. The score drops; the model has not learned anything new.
    \item \textbf{No theory of the goal.} There is no principle that says what a debiased model should \emph{do}, only what it should not do. Supervision is shaped by whichever benchmark the method was built for.
\end{itemize}

The deeper issue behind all three is that the field treats bias in language models as a property to be erased, while ignoring what the science of bias reduction in \emph{humans} already knows. Humans are biased too, and decades of social psychology have studied how that bias gets reduced. The answer is not awareness, suppression, or restraint; it is concrete cognitive practices that change how a person processes social information \citep{allport1954nature, devine2012longterm, galinsky2000perspective}. The prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm} identifies five such practices, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, each describing, in operational steps, what to attend to, what assumption to override, and how to respond. These are procedures, not principles. That is what makes them useful to us: a procedure can be taught.

We introduce \diy{} (DIY), which teaches these five interventions to language models as a single supervision protocol. The same intervention-labeled data drives three composable techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} delivers the interventions as in-context demonstrations, \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} delivers them as instruction-tuning targets, and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} delivers them as a guided second pass over the model's first response. We make no claim about model cognition; only that the procedure transfers.

Across multiple bias benchmarks, debiasing baselines, reasoning benchmarks, and model families, \diy{} consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with the largest gains when \textsc{Teach} is composed with \textsc{Revise}, and reasoning competence is preserved.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
    \item We reframe bias mitigation as teaching a target \emph{behavior}, grounded in prejudice-reduction research from social psychology, rather than suppressing a benchmark symptom.
    \item We introduce \diy{}, a single supervision protocol over five validated cognitive interventions, exposed through three composable techniques: \textsc{Show}, \textsc{Teach}, \textsc{Revise}.
    \item We construct an intervention-labeled data pipeline that drives all three techniques from one supervision signal across social categories and benchmark formats.
    \item We show that \diy{} reduces bias and beats baselines without eroding reasoning, across benchmarks, baselines, model families, and scales.
\end{enumerate}
