\begin{abstract}
Social bias in large language models is a well-documented harm, and many mitigations have been proposed, but each typically targets a single dataset, task, or bias dimension, teaching the model to \emph{suppress} a symptom rather than to \emph{reduce} bias as a behavior. LLMs are trained on human text and inherit human bias, yet decades of social-psychology research already show \emph{how} prejudice can be reduced in people through validated cognitive interventions. We bring that literature into model training and inference, teaching models the same bias-reduction strategies so that debiasing becomes a transferable skill rather than a benchmark-specific patch. We introduce \diy{} (DIY), which operationalizes five established prejudice-reducing interventions, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, through three complementary techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} (in-context demonstrations), \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} (instruction tuning), and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} (intervention-guided self-revision). Evaluated against a broad set of debiasing baselines on bias and reasoning benchmarks, \diy{} consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with gains that generalize across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions, showing that grounding mitigation in transferable cognitive behavior beats dataset-specific suppression. Our code and data are available here\footnote{\url{https://github.com/chahatraj/DebiasItYourself}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Existing approaches to bias in language models predominantly target specific failures, individual stereotyped associations, biased coreference resolutions, or skewed question-answering pairs, with a dedicated mitigation per failure type \citep{bolukbasi2016man, caliskan2017semantics, blodgett2020language, sheng2019woman, parrish-etal-2022-bbq, bender2021dangers, weidinger2022taxonomy}. We propose an alternative framing. We treat bias not as an enumerable set of output failures, but as a regularity in how a model processes bias-sensitive input. The corresponding objective is not to remove particular biased outputs, but to teach the model a uniform procedure for handling social information.

This reframing, from correcting individual biases to teaching a general procedure for treating bias-sensitive input, is the central idea of this work. To our knowledge, bias mitigation has not previously been articulated in this form, and we present this paper as a first step in its study: stating the idea, providing a concrete instantiation, and evaluating whether that instantiation produces the predicted effects.

Existing mitigation methods, including debiased embeddings \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender}, counterfactual augmentation \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}, prompt-based steering \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}, fairness-aware fine-tuning and preference optimization \citep{liang2020towards, ouyang2022training}, and decoding-time constraints \citep{schick2021self}, all adopt the per-failure view. Three limitations follow from that framing:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Per-bias supervision.} Methods are calibrated to a particular dataset, task format, or social dimension; a model debiased for gender on a coreference task is not, in any guaranteed sense, debiased for race on question answering. The set of biases to be addressed grows with each new evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Output suppression rather than behavioral change.} Many methods reduce bias scores by inducing vague, evasive, or refusing responses, leaving unchanged the underlying treatment of social cues \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}.
    \item \textbf{Absence of a positive specification.} Existing work specifies what a model should not produce, but does not specify what a debiased model should produce when it encounters bias-sensitive input. Supervision is therefore determined by the available benchmark rather than by an account of how such input should be processed.
\end{itemize}

To teach a procedure for handling social information, a candidate procedure is required. The relevant body of work is in social psychology, where the reduction of bias in human cognition has been studied for several decades. The consistent empirical finding is that bias reduction is achieved not through awareness or restraint, but through specific cognitive practices that change how social information is processed at the moment of encounter \citep{allport1954nature, devine2012longterm, galinsky2000perspective}. The prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm} identifies five such practices, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, each specifying, in operational terms, what to attend to, which assumption to override, and how to respond. It is the procedural form of these practices, rather than their psychological content, that makes them a suitable target for supervised learning.

We introduce \diy{} (DIY), a concrete instantiation of the proposed framing. \diy{} teaches the five interventions to a language model under a single supervision protocol, so that the model applies the same procedure across tasks. The same intervention-labeled data is delivered through three composable techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} provides in-context demonstrations of the interventions; \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} provides instruction-tuning targets derived from the same supervision; and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} provides a guided second pass over the model's first response. We make no claim regarding model cognition; we claim only that the procedural form transfers.

We evaluate \diy{} against debiasing baselines on multiple bias benchmarks, on reasoning benchmarks intended to detect competence loss, and across model families and scales. \diy{} consistently reduces bias relative to baselines, with the largest reductions when \textsc{Teach} is composed with \textsc{Revise}, and reasoning performance is preserved. These results provide initial evidence that teaching a procedure for handling bias-sensitive input, rather than correcting its individual outputs, is a viable direction for bias mitigation.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{Framing.} We reframe bias mitigation as teaching a model a general procedure for treating bias-sensitive input, in place of correcting individual biases.
    \item \textbf{Method.} We introduce \diy{}, a first concrete instantiation of this framing, grounded in five validated cognitive interventions from social psychology and delivered through three composable techniques: \textsc{Show}, \textsc{Teach}, \textsc{Revise}.
    \item \textbf{Pipeline.} We construct an intervention-labeled data pipeline that drives all three techniques from one supervision signal across social categories and benchmark formats.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation.} We show that \diy{} reduces bias and outperforms baselines without degrading reasoning, across benchmarks, baselines, model families, and scales.
\end{enumerate}
