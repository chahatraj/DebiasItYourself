\begin{abstract}
Social bias in large language models is a well-documented harm, and many mitigation strategies have been proposed---debiased embeddings, counterfactual data augmentation, prompt-based steering, fairness-aware fine-tuning, and benchmark-specific filters. These solutions share a structural limitation: each addresses bias on a particular dataset, a particular task, or a single bias dimension, and the gains rarely transfer beyond the slice they were designed for. In effect, existing methods teach a model to \emph{suppress} a specific symptom of bias rather than teach it \emph{how} to reduce bias as a behavior. We argue this is the wrong unit of supervision. LLMs are trained on human-produced text; humans are biased, and so models inherit that bias---but humans also have decades of social-psychology research on \emph{how} prejudice can be reduced, in the form of cognitive interventions validated in controlled studies. We propose to bring that literature into model training and inference: rather than narrow point-fixes, we teach models the same bias-reduction strategies that work for people, so that debiasing becomes a transferable skill rather than a benchmark-specific patch. Concretely, we introduce \textsc{Debias It Yourself} (DIY), a framework that operationalizes five established prejudice-reducing interventions from social psychology---Stereotype Replacement, Counter-stereotypic Imaging, Individuation, Perspective-taking, and Positive Contact---into a unified supervision schema, instantiated through three complementary mechanisms: \emph{Show} (in-context demonstrations of intervention use), \emph{Teach} (instruction tuning on intervention-grounded supervision), and \emph{Revise} (intervention-guided second-pass self-revision). We evaluate DIY across a range of bias benchmarks against a broad set of debiasing baselines, and on reasoning benchmarks to verify that mitigation does not erode task competence. Across model families and scales, DIY consistently lowers bias error and outperforms existing baselines, with the most stable gains when supervised teaching is composed with intervention-guided revision---evidence that grounding mitigation in transferable cognitive behavior, rather than dataset-specific suppression, generalizes across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions.
\end{abstract}
