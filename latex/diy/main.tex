\begin{abstract}
Social bias in large language models is a well-documented harm, and many mitigations have been proposed, but each typically targets a single dataset, task, or bias dimension, teaching the model to \emph{suppress} a symptom rather than to \emph{reduce} bias as a behavior. LLMs are trained on human text and inherit human bias, yet decades of social-psychology research already show \emph{how} prejudice can be reduced in people through validated cognitive interventions. We bring that literature into model training and inference, teaching models the same bias-reduction strategies so that debiasing becomes a transferable skill rather than a benchmark-specific patch. We introduce \diy{} (DIY), which operationalizes five established prejudice-reducing interventions, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, through three complementary techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} (in-context demonstrations), \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} (instruction tuning), and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} (intervention-guided self-revision). Evaluated against a broad set of debiasing baselines on bias and reasoning benchmarks, \diy{} consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with gains that generalize across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions, showing that grounding mitigation in transferable cognitive behavior beats dataset-specific suppression. Our code and data are available here\footnote{\url{https://github.com/chahatraj/DebiasItYourself}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models inherit social bias from the human-produced text they are trained on, treating names, demographic descriptors, occupations, and institutional contexts as task evidence even when these cues are irrelevant to the correct answer \citep{bolukbasi2016man, caliskan2017semantics, blodgett2020language, sheng2019woman, parrish-etal-2022-bbq}. As LLMs reach hiring, education, healthcare, and content moderation, the resulting failures fall disproportionately on already-marginalized groups \citep{bender2021dangers, weidinger2022taxonomy}.

A large literature attempts to mitigate this through debiased embeddings \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender}, counterfactual augmentation \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}, prompt-based steering \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}, fairness-aware fine-tuning and preference optimization \citep{liang2020towards, ouyang2022training}, and decoding-time constraints \citep{schick2021self}. These methods share three limitations: (i) each is tuned to a single dataset, task, or bias dimension, and gains rarely transfer beyond that slice; (ii) they teach the model to \emph{suppress} a symptom rather than to \emph{reduce} bias as a behavior; and (iii) they offer no principled account of \emph{what} bias-reducing behavior the model should learn, beyond a benchmark target.

LLMs inherit bias from biased human text, but humans have decades of social-psychology research on \emph{how} prejudice is reduced in people through validated cognitive interventions \citep{devine2012longterm, allport1954nature, galinsky2000perspective}. The prejudice habit-breaking intervention of \citet{devine2012longterm} specifies five such strategies, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, each defining what evidence to attend to, what heuristic to avoid, and what task-consistent response to produce. We treat these as a transferable behavioral schema a language model can be taught to apply (not a claim about model cognition; the procedure transfers, not the psychology).

We introduce \diy{} (DIY), a structured supervision framework that operationalizes the five interventions into a single protocol for training and inference, exposed through three composable techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} provides intervention-grounded in-context demonstrations, \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} converts the same supervision into instruction-tuning targets, and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} uses an intervention-guided second pass to repair the model's first response.

We evaluate \diy{} against a broad set of debiasing baselines on multiple bias benchmarks, and on reasoning benchmarks that test whether mitigation erodes task competence, across model families and scales. \diy{} consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with the largest gains when \textsc{Teach} is composed with \textsc{Revise}, and the pattern holds across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
    \item We reframe bias mitigation as teaching a transferable bias-reducing \emph{behavior}, grounded in prejudice-reduction theory from social psychology.
    \item We introduce \diy{}, a single supervision protocol over five validated cognitive interventions, exposed through three composable techniques: \textsc{Show}, \textsc{Teach}, \textsc{Revise}.
    \item We construct an intervention-labeled data pipeline that drives all three techniques from one supervision signal across social categories and benchmark formats.
    \item We show empirically that \diy{} lowers bias and beats baselines without eroding reasoning, with gains that generalize across benchmarks, baselines, model families, and scales.
\end{enumerate}
