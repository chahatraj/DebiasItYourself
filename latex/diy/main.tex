\begin{abstract}
Social bias in large language models is a well-documented harm, and many mitigations have been proposed, but each typically targets a single dataset, task, or bias dimension, teaching the model to \emph{suppress} a symptom rather than to \emph{reduce} bias as a behavior. LLMs are trained on human text and inherit human bias, yet decades of social-psychology research already show \emph{how} prejudice can be reduced in people through validated cognitive interventions. We bring that literature into model training and inference, teaching models the same bias-reduction strategies so that debiasing becomes a transferable skill rather than a benchmark-specific patch. We introduce \diy{} (DIY), which operationalizes five established prejudice-reducing interventions, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, through three complementary techniques: \textsc{Show} (in-context demonstrations), \textsc{Train} (instruction tuning), and \textsc{Revise} (intervention-guided self-revision). Evaluated against a broad set of debiasing baselines on bias and reasoning benchmarks, DIY consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with gains that generalize across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions, showing that grounding mitigation in transferable cognitive behavior beats dataset-specific suppression. Our code and data are available here\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/DebiasItYourself}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Prejudice is a habit that can be broken \citep{devine2012longterm}. A central finding of social psychology is that bias does not arise from a lack of correct beliefs or a failure to inhibit individual responses; it arises from the routine, largely automatic way in which social information is processed at the moment it is encountered. It is reduced not by suppressing individual outputs, but by trained cognitive practices that alter this processing itself \citep{allport1954nature, devine2012longterm, galinsky2000perspective}. Language models, trained predominantly on human-produced text, inherit similar patterns, with socially salient cues routinely treated as task evidence even when they are not. Most existing mitigation work on language models, however, addresses bias as a property of model outputs through targeted data, model edits, or output-time constraints, with each intervention scoped to a particular dataset, social dimension, or task format \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender, zmigrod2019counterfactual, schick2021self, liang2020towards}. Bias reductions reported on benchmarks consequently capture changes in output behavior conditional on the evaluation distribution, rather than changes in the model's general handling of bias-sensitive input.

Most existing work on bias mitigation in language models falls into a small set of strategies. \emph{Representation-level methods} project or fine-tune learned embeddings to reduce association between protected attributes and downstream targets \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender, liang2020towards}. \emph{Data-centric methods} construct counterfactual or balanced training data to dilute biased correlations during pretraining or fine-tuning \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}. \emph{Inference-time methods} use prompts, instructions, or self-correction to steer generated outputs away from biased content \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}, and \emph{alignment-based methods} fold fairness signals into preference optimization or reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{ouyang2022training}.

Three limitations recur across these strategies. \textbf{1.} Supervision is calibrated to a particular dataset, task, or social dimension, and the resulting mitigation does not transfer to others. \textbf{2.} Lower bias scores are often obtained through vague or refusal-style outputs, leaving the model's handling of bias-sensitive input unchanged \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}. \textbf{3.} Existing supervision specifies what the model should not produce on a benchmark, but not how bias-sensitive input should be processed when it is encountered.

We propose \diy{} (\textsc{DIY}), a bias mitigation framework that targets the procedure by which a language model handles bias-sensitive input. \textsc{DIY} draws on social psychology, where bias reduction has been studied empirically for decades, and is consistently achieved through trained cognitive practices that change how social information is processed when it is encountered. \textsc{DIY} operationalizes the five practices of the prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm}, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, into a single intervention protocol. \textsc{DIY} derives three composable techniques: \textsc{Show}, in-context demonstrations of the interventions; \textsc{Train}, instruction-tuning targets derived from the same protocol; and \textsc{Revise}, a guided second pass over the model's first response.

We evaluate \textsc{DIY} against debiasing baselines on multiple bias benchmarks, on reasoning benchmarks, and across model families and scales. \textsc{DIY} consistently reduces bias relative to baselines, with the largest reductions when \textsc{Train} is composed with \textsc{Revise}. These results provide evidence that teaching a procedure for handling bias-sensitive input, rather than correcting its individual outputs, is a viable direction for bias mitigation.

We organize the paper around three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.},leftmargin=2.8em,labelwidth=2.4em,labelsep=0.4em,align=left,itemsep=0pt,parsep=3pt,topsep=3pt,partopsep=0pt]
    \item Can a language model internalize a bias-reducing procedure as a transferable behavior, rather than as benchmark-specific corrections?
    \item Does the resulting model retain its performance on standard reasoning and instruction-following benchmarks?
    \item Does the learned procedure generalize across held-out bias dimensions, task formats, and model families?
\end{enumerate}

Our work makes the following key contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0pt,parsep=3pt,topsep=3pt,partopsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Task.} A reformulation of bias mitigation in language models, in which the supervision target is the procedure by which the model handles bias-sensitive input rather than the correction of individual biased outputs.
    \item \textbf{Method.} \textsc{DIY}, a unified intervention protocol that operationalizes the five practices of the prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm} and is delivered through three composable techniques, \textsc{Show}, \textsc{Train}, and \textsc{Revise}.
    \item \textbf{Key finding.} \textsc{DIY} yields consistent reductions in bias across multiple bias benchmarks and social dimensions, outperforms a range of debiasing baselines, and preserves reasoning competence, with the effect holding across model families and scales.
    \item \textbf{Data.} A dataset of bias-sensitive instances paired with intervention-labeled debiased targets, spanning multiple social categories and benchmark formats, providing a single supervision signal for all three techniques in \textsc{DIY}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Bias mitigation in language models.} Existing work falls into four families: \emph{representation-level} methods that project out or fine-tune attribute subspaces \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender, liang2020towards}; \emph{data-centric} methods that augment training with counterfactual or balanced examples \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}; \emph{inference-time} methods that prompt or self-correct toward fairer outputs \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}; and \emph{alignment-based} methods that incorporate fairness signals into preference optimization \citep{ouyang2022training}. All four supervise from a particular benchmark or social dimension, and gains reflect output-level adjustments on the targeted distribution. The benchmarks themselves reinforce this view, scoring stereotype-pair preferences \citep{nangia-etal-2020-crows, nadeem-etal-2021-stereoset}, ambiguous QA \citep{parrish-etal-2022-bbq}, counterfactual coreference \citep{rudinger-etal-2018-gender}, and harmful completion \citep{nozza-etal-2021-honest, smith-etal-2022-im} on outputs, so supervision derived from them inherits the same form.

\paragraph{Cognitive framings in NLP.} A smaller line of work borrows psychology-inspired framings: self-debiasing prompts a model to suppress its own biased generations \citep{schick2021self}, moral self-correction uses instruction-following to elicit fairer responses \citep{ganguli2023capacity}, \citet{raj2024breaking} apply the contact hypothesis to debias LLMs, and \citet{xu-etal-2024-walking} use perspective-taking prompts. These methods each operationalize a single intervention; supervision remains defined relative to a target output, and the connection to social-psychology research on prejudice reduction has remained largely metaphorical.

\paragraph{Bias reduction in human cognition.} Human bias reduction has been studied empirically for decades. \citet{allport1954nature} established that structured intergroup contact reduces prejudice, \citet{galinsky2000perspective} showed that perspective-taking reduces stereotype expression, and \citet{devine2012longterm} integrated these into a habit-breaking intervention based on five practices, with sustained reductions in implicit race bias at follow-up. The consistent finding is that bias reduction comes from changing how social information is processed at the moment of encounter, not from suppressing individual responses. This mechanism has not been carried over to language-model supervision.

\paragraph{Position of this work.} \diylabel{} addresses the limitations above. It supervises across benchmarks rather than per-benchmark; targets the procedure by which the model handles bias-sensitive input rather than the output; and grounds supervision in the five operationally specified practices of \citet{devine2012longterm} rather than psychology-inspired vocabulary, while making no claim about model cognition.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data-methods}

\subsection{Intervention Strategies}
\label{sec:strategies}

Our debiasing approach is grounded in a set of five cognitively motivated intervention strategies derived from established findings in social and cognitive psychology. Each strategy targets a distinct mechanism through which stereotypes and social biases arise in reasoning, and is operationalized as a structured, step-wise reasoning process. Importantly, these strategies are designed to be applied independently or composed, and are agnostic to model architecture, task, or domain. We adopt the five practices from the prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm} because they form an empirically validated intervention with sustained effects on implicit bias, and because they are specified as explicit step-wise procedures, which makes them suitable for direct operationalization as model-facing supervision.

\paragraph{\srlabel{}.}
This practice disrupts biased reasoning by explicitly identifying a stereotype and substituting it with a fairer, individualized interpretation. It proceeds in three steps: (i) recognizing whether a stereotype or bias is being invoked, (ii) reflecting on why the stereotype may be inaccurate, overgeneralized, or harmful, and (iii) replacing the biased assumption with a neutral or evidence-based alternative. The goal is not to suppress reasoning, but to redirect it away from group-based generalizations.

\paragraph{\cilabel{}.}
This practice reduces bias by strengthening associations that contradict prevailing stereotypes. After identifying the stereotype being invoked, the model is prompted to imagine a real or hypothetical individual who defies the stereotype and to elaborate concrete details about this counter-stereotypic example. By reinforcing alternative associations, the strategy weakens the automatic activation of stereotypical beliefs during reasoning.

\paragraph{\indlabel{}.}
This practice shifts attention from social categories to individual-specific information. It instructs the model to consciously decenter group membership, gather relevant individuating details such as personal traits, behaviors, or context, and revise the initial judgment based on this information. The resulting behavior mitigates bias by replacing category-based inference with case-specific reasoning.

\paragraph{\ptlabel{}.}
This practice encourages the model to adopt the viewpoint of the person being stereotyped. It involves simulating the thoughts, feelings, or experiences of the targeted individual and integrating this perspective into the final response. By foregrounding lived experience, the strategy reframes biased assumptions and promotes more empathetic and context-aware reasoning.

\paragraph{\pclabel{}.}
This practice draws on contact theory, which suggests that meaningful positive interactions reduce prejudice. It prompts the model to imagine or recall a constructive interaction with a member of the stereotyped group, describe the interaction in detail, and generalize its implications to challenge the original stereotype. The emphasis is on affective engagement rather than factual correction alone.

Together, these five strategies form a modular cognitive intervention toolkit. Each strategy can be deployed independently, enabling fine-grained analysis of their individual effects, or combined to create stronger debiasing interventions. Crucially, all strategies operate at the level of reasoning and interpretation, rather than relying on task-specific labels or outcome supervision.

\subsection{Data Creation}
\label{sec:data}

We construct a synthetic debiasing dataset using a self-instruct-style generation pipeline grounded in the five-step cognitive intervention framework. The dataset consists of paired biased inputs and debiased outputs, where each biased input is realized in three textual forms, an \emph{opinion} (a direct biased statement, e.g., \emph{``All [identity] are dishonest''}), a biased \emph{action} (a sentence in which the opinion drives a discriminatory behavior), and a biased \emph{event} (a sentence describing an event that leads to forming the opinion), and each debiased output follows a specific cognitive debiasing strategy. The resulting data is used both for supervised instruction tuning and for inference-time in-context debiasing.

\paragraph{Seed Identity Generation.}
We first generate a diverse set of demographic identity descriptors that serve as the subjects of potential bias. These include both single-identity configurations (e.g., \emph{``a Black man''}) and paired-identity configurations (e.g., \emph{``a Black man and a White man''}), enabling the construction of bias scenarios involving individual and comparative judgments. Identities are sampled across multiple demographic dimensions and are intentionally generated independently of any specific task or outcome.

\paragraph{Bias Concept Generation.}
Given a set of identity descriptors, we next generate bias concepts that reflect commonly observed stereotypical traits, assumptions, or associations (e.g., perceived competence, socioeconomic status, athletic ability). These concepts are generated without attaching ground-truth labels or correctness constraints, and are intended solely to elicit biased reasoning when combined with identity information. Bias concepts may target a single individual or implicitly contrast multiple identities.

\paragraph{Biased Instance Generation.}
Biased instances are created by combining identity descriptors and bias concepts within open-ended scenarios, such as professional, social, or everyday contexts. A language model is prompted to synthesize these components into coherent natural language inputs designed to plausibly trigger stereotypical reasoning. This step yields bias-prone prompts without explicitly instructing the model to behave unfairly, mirroring how bias arises in unconstrained language use.

\paragraph{Debiased Instance Generation.}
For each biased instance, we generate a corresponding debiased output using a specified cognitive intervention strategy. The model is prompted to apply the selected strategy by explicitly following its step-wise reasoning structure (e.g., recognizing the stereotype, reflecting on its limitations, and revising the interpretation). This results in aligned biased--debiased pairs that can be formatted as instruction--response examples for supervised fine-tuning or as demonstrations for in-context debiasing at inference time.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:implementations}

\diylabel{} delivers the cognitive strategies of Section~\ref{sec:strategies} to a language model through three composable techniques: \showlabel{}, \trainlabel{}, and \reviselabel{}.

\paragraph{\showlabel{}.}
This inference interface places the strategy and a small number of biased--debiased example pairs (worked through the strategy's three steps) in the prompt as in-context demonstrations. The model conditions on the demonstrations and produces a debiased response under the same step structure, without parameter updates. We evaluate it \emph{per strategy}: each of the five cognitive strategies is run as a separate condition, and we additionally report an aggregated \textsc{All-strategies} setting that mixes the five.

\paragraph{\trainlabel{}.}
This training interface converts the intervention-labeled biased--debiased pairs into instruction-tuning targets and uses them to fine-tune the model with parameter-efficient adaptation. After training, the model can apply the strategy without explicit demonstrations or strategy text in the prompt.

\paragraph{\reviselabel{}.}
This second-pass interface first lets the model produce an unconstrained response to the input, after which the model is prompted to apply a selected strategy to its own response by following the same three steps. The revised response replaces the first. As with \showlabel{}, this interface is evaluated per strategy and in the \textsc{All-strategies} aggregation. When it is composed with \trainlabel{} (\diytrainreviselabel{}), the revision pass is run both on the fine-tuned model and on the base model, allowing us to disentangle the contribution of training from that of intervention-guided revision. When \showlabel{} is composed with \trainlabel{} (\diytrainshowlabel{}), the in-context demonstrations are drawn from the same strategy that the fine-tune was trained on.

The three techniques are independent in delivery and compatible in supervision: all three are driven by the same intervention-labeled data of Section~\ref{sec:data}, so any composition of \showlabel{}, \trainlabel{}, and \reviselabel{} continues to operate over the same five strategies.

\paragraph{Evaluated configurations.}
We evaluate \diylabel{} across the cells of the $\textsc{Show}\times\textsc{Train}\times\textsc{Revise}$ space, naming each configuration by the techniques it activates. The single-mechanism configurations are \diytrainlabel{} (instruction tuning alone), \diyshowlabel{} (in-context demonstrations alone), and \diyreviselabel{} (intervention-guided second pass alone); the two-mechanism configurations are \diytrainshowlabel{} (training combined with demonstrations) and \diytrainreviselabel{} (training combined with revision); and the unmodified baseline is denoted \baselabel{}. For every configuration that involves a prompt component, we additionally run a 0-shot and a 1-shot variant, where the shot count is the number of in-context demonstrations of the selected cognitive strategy.

\paragraph{Training and supervision scale.}
For \trainlabel{}, we train one fine-tuned checkpoint per (strategy, instance-type) cell of the data, plus an \textsc{All-strategies} checkpoint that combines all five. Each checkpoint is trained on a 500-example subset (sampled with a fixed seed) for parity across cells, using LoRA adaptation on \llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct (rank $256$, $\alpha=256$, learning rate $2{\times}10^{-4}$, three epochs).

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:experimental-results}

We evaluate \diy{} on \llama{}-3.1-8B-Instruct, \llama{}-3.1-70B-Instruct, and \qwen{}-2.5-32B-Instruct, using five bias benchmarks: CrowS-Pairs \citep{nangia-etal-2020-crows}, StereoSet \citep{nadeem-etal-2021-stereoset}, BBQ \citep{parrish-etal-2022-bbq}, WinoBias \citep{zhao-etal-2018-gender}, and WinoGender \citep{rudinger-etal-2018-gender}; and three reasoning benchmarks: Balanced COPA \citep{kavumba-etal-2019-choosing}, ARC-Challenge, and ARC-Easy \citep{clark2018arc}. We compare against ten prior debiasing methods spanning three families. \emph{Prompt-based} methods elicit fairer responses through prompt engineering: SelfDebias \citep{gallegos2024selfdebiasing} and RSB \citep{kamruzzaman2025rsb}. \emph{Training-based} methods modify model parameters: DebiasNLG \citep{wang-demberg-2024-parameter}, DebiasLLMs \citep{dong2024disclosure}, PEFT \citep{zhao-etal-2025-debiasing}, DPO \citep{dai2025mitigating}, LFTF \citep{qin2025lftf}, and BiasEdit \citep{xu-etal-2025-biasedit}. \emph{Inference-based} methods steer or correct outputs at decoding time: FairSteer \citep{li2025fairsteer}, CAL \citep{sun-etal-2024-causal}, and BBA \citep{lin2026bba}. Implementation details for \diy{} are provided in the appendix.

\subsection{Bias Mitigation}
\label{sec:rq1}

Because the five bias benchmarks differ in their native scoring conventions, we report two normalized quantities that are commensurable across them. The \emph{bias score} on each benchmark, $\mathrm{score} = |\mathrm{score}_{\text{benchmark}} - \mathrm{score}_{\text{unbiased}}|$, where $\mathrm{score}_{\text{unbiased}}$ is the value an unbiased model would produce ($50$ for CrowS-Pairs and StereoSet, $0$ for BBQ, WinoBias, and WinoGender). The \emph{average rank} of each method, computed by ranking all methods on each benchmark by bias score (rank $1$ = lowest) and averaging across benchmarks: $\mathrm{avg\,rank}(m) = \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{b \in \mathcal{B}} \mathrm{rank}_b(m)$, where $\mathcal{B}$ is the set of bias benchmarks. Both quantities are lower-is-better.

\paragraph{Cross-benchmark performance.}
On every tested model, the lowest average rank across the five bias benchmarks is achieved by a \diylabel{} configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-rank}). \diytrainreviselabel{} ($3.83$) leads on \llama{}-3.1-8B, and \diyreviselabel{} leads on both \qwen{}-2.5-32B ($3.50$) and \llama{}-3.1-70B ($4.83$). The lead is consistent across model families and scales: intervention-guided revision (\diyreviselabel{}, \diytrainreviselabel{}) occupies both top-two slots on every model. The strongest non-\diylabel{} baselines (DebiasLLMs, PEFT, BiasEdit, SelfDebias) trail the leading \diylabel{} configuration by an average rank of $1.5$ to $2.3$ on the smaller models, narrowing to $1.0$ on \llama{}-3.1-70B.

\paragraph{Method Comparison.}
Among the five \diylabel{} configurations, intervention-guided revision is the dominant lever, and combining it with instruction tuning gives the strongest overall configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:debiasing-shot-llama8b}; \llama{}-3.1-70B and \qwen{}-2.5-32B in Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:app-debiasing-shot-llama70b}--\ref{fig:app-debiasing-shot-qwen}). \diyreviselabel{} reduces bias substantially across all three models, with \llama{}-3.1-8B CrowS-Pairs dropping from $15.2$ at \baselabel{} to $2.45$, and composing it with \diytrainlabel{} as \diytrainreviselabel{} sharpens this further (CrowS-Pairs: $0.860$). \diytrainlabel{} on its own offers limited improvement, suggesting parameter-level supervision is most useful when paired with an inference-time procedure rather than as a standalone fix. \diyshowlabel{} behaves differently: $0$-shot demonstrations are competitive with the leading configurations, while adding more demonstrations does not consistently help. Shot effects are technique-dependent more broadly: revision benefits modestly from additional demonstrations, while training-based configurations are largely insensitive to shot count. The picture across configurations is consistent with the central claim: an effective \diylabel{} setup combines an intervention-guided procedure at inference with parameter-level supervision that supports it.

\begin{insightbox}
\notebookpencil~\textbf{Takeaway 2.} Intervention-guided revision is the strongest single technique, and pairing it with instruction tuning yields the most reliable configuration. Instruction tuning alone offers limited improvement; in-context demonstrations are most effective at zero-shot.
\end{insightbox}

\paragraph{Bias--reasoning tradeoff.}
A central concern for any debiasing method is that bias reductions should not come at the cost of general task competence. \diylabel{} configurations sit on the favorable corner of the bias--reasoning frontier across all three models, achieving low mean bias without the reasoning collapse seen in several baselines (Figure~\ref{fig:bias-reasoning-pareto}). On \llama{}-3.1-8B, \diyreviselabel{} and \diytrainreviselabel{} reach the lowest mean bias of any method while preserving reasoning accuracy near $70\%$, in contrast to DebiasLLMs, which reaches a similar bias level only by dropping reasoning below $50\%$. The same pattern holds on \qwen{}-2.5-32B, where \diytrainreviselabel{} achieves both the highest reasoning accuracy ($\sim 93\%$) and a mean bias comparable to the strongest debiasing baselines, while BiasEdit and DebiasLLMs reach low bias only at substantially lower reasoning. On \llama{}-3.1-70B, \diyreviselabel{} and \diytrainreviselabel{} again sit in the top-left region, while baselines that match their bias level (DebiasLLMs, BiasEdit) collapse to reasoning accuracy below $40\%$. The gap is structural: methods that suppress bias by inducing vague or refusal-style outputs lose the ability to answer task questions correctly, whereas \diylabel{} preserves it because the supervision target is intervention-guided behavior on bias-sensitive input rather than output suppression.

\begin{insightbox}
\notebookpencil~\textbf{Takeaway 3.} \diylabel{} sits on the favorable corner of the bias--reasoning frontier on every tested model: it matches the lowest-bias baselines without their reasoning collapse, supporting the claim that reducing bias as a behavior is compatible with preserving general task competence.
\end{insightbox}

\section{Strategy Examples}
\label{sec:strategy-examples}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=diyStrategyBg!35, colframe=diyBadgeBorder, title=Stereotype Replacement]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Recognize:} This statement relies on a gender stereotype that equates emotional expression with poor leadership ability.

\textbf{Reflect:} This generalization is inaccurate and overlooks evidence that emotional intelligence can enhance leadership effectiveness.

\textbf{Replace:} Leadership ability depends on skills, experience, and judgment, not gender.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=diyStrategyBg!35, colframe=diyBadgeBorder, title=Counter-Stereotype Imaging]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Recognize:} This statement invokes a stereotype that questions women's suitability for leadership.

\textbf{Imagine:} Consider Jacinda Ardern, former Prime Minister of New Zealand.

\textbf{Reinforce:} She demonstrated calm, decisive leadership during major crises while also leading with empathy, contradicting the stereotype.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=diyStrategyBg!35, colframe=diyBadgeBorder, title=Individuating]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Attend:} This statement applies a broad group-based judgment rather than evaluating an individual.

\textbf{Gather:} Angela Merkel served as Chancellor of Germany for 16 years and was widely recognized for her pragmatic and steady leadership.

\textbf{Adjust:} Evaluating leadership based on individual evidence contradicts the stereotype and supports fairer judgment.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=diyStrategyBg!35, colframe=diyBadgeBorder, title=Perspective Taking]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Adopt:} Take the perspective of a woman in a leadership role facing recurring skepticism.

\textbf{Simulate:} She may find that assertiveness is labeled as emotional, while similar behavior in male peers is praised, creating unequal expectations.

\textbf{Integrate:} From this perspective, the stereotype reflects bias rather than leadership ability.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=diyStrategyBg!35, colframe=diyBadgeBorder, title=Positive Contact]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Imagine:} Recall a positive professional interaction with a woman leading a high-pressure team.

\textbf{Engage:} She acknowledged team concerns while clearly guiding decisions, improving focus and morale.

\textbf{Extend:} This experience shows that emotional awareness can be a leadership strength, challenging the stereotype.
\end{tcolorbox}
