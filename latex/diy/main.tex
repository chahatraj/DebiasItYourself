\begin{abstract}
Social bias in large language models is a well-documented harm, and many mitigations have been proposed, but each typically targets a single dataset, task, or bias dimension, teaching the model to \emph{suppress} a symptom rather than to \emph{reduce} bias as a behavior. LLMs are trained on human text and inherit human bias, yet decades of social-psychology research already show \emph{how} prejudice can be reduced in people through validated cognitive interventions. We bring that literature into model training and inference, teaching models the same bias-reduction strategies so that debiasing becomes a transferable skill rather than a benchmark-specific patch. We introduce \diy{} (DIY), which operationalizes five established prejudice-reducing interventions, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, through three complementary techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}} (in-context demonstrations), \textbf{\textsc{Teach}} (instruction tuning), and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}} (intervention-guided self-revision). Evaluated against a broad set of debiasing baselines on bias and reasoning benchmarks, \diy{} consistently lowers bias and outperforms prior methods, with gains that generalize across tasks, models, scales, and bias dimensions, showing that grounding mitigation in transferable cognitive behavior beats dataset-specific suppression. Our code and data are available here\footnote{\url{https://github.com/chahatraj/DebiasItYourself}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Prejudice is a habit that can be broken \citep{devine2012longterm}. A central finding of social psychology is that bias does not arise from a lack of correct beliefs or a failure to inhibit individual responses; it arises from the routine, largely automatic way in which social information is processed at the moment it is encountered. It is reduced not by suppressing individual outputs, but by trained cognitive practices that alter this processing itself \citep{allport1954nature, devine2012longterm, galinsky2000perspective}. Language models, trained predominantly on human-produced text, inherit similar patterns, with socially salient cues routinely treated as task evidence even when they are not. Most existing mitigation work on language models, however, addresses bias as a property of model outputs through targeted data, model edits, or output-time constraints, with each intervention scoped to a particular dataset, social dimension, or task format \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender, zmigrod2019counterfactual, schick2021self, liang2020towards}. Bias reductions reported on benchmarks consequently capture changes in output behavior conditional on the evaluation distribution, rather than changes in the model's general handling of bias-sensitive input.

Most existing work on bias mitigation in language models falls into a small set of strategies. \emph{Representation-level methods} project or fine-tune learned embeddings to reduce association between protected attributes and downstream targets \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender, liang2020towards}. \emph{Data-centric methods} construct counterfactual or balanced training data to dilute biased correlations during pretraining or fine-tuning \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}. \emph{Inference-time methods} use prompts, instructions, or self-correction to steer generated outputs away from biased content \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}, and \emph{alignment-based methods} fold fairness signals into preference optimization or reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{ouyang2022training}.

Three limitations recur across these strategies. \textbf{1.} Supervision is calibrated to a particular dataset, task, or social dimension, and the resulting mitigation does not transfer to others. \textbf{2.} Lower bias scores are often obtained through vague or refusal-style outputs, leaving the model's handling of bias-sensitive input unchanged \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}. \textbf{3.} Existing supervision specifies what the model should not produce on a benchmark, but not how bias-sensitive input should be processed when it is encountered.

We propose \diy{} (DIY), a bias mitigation framework that targets the procedure by which a language model handles bias-sensitive input. \diy{} draws on social psychology, where bias reduction has been studied empirically for decades, and is consistently achieved through trained cognitive practices that change how social information is processed when it is encountered. \diy{} operationalizes the five practices of the prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm}, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, into a single intervention protocol. \diy{} derives three composable techniques: \textbf{\textsc{Show}}, in-context demonstrations of the interventions; \textbf{\textsc{Teach}}, instruction-tuning targets derived from the same protocol; and \textbf{\textsc{Revise}}, a guided second pass over the model's first response.

We evaluate \diy{} against debiasing baselines on multiple bias benchmarks, on reasoning benchmarks, and across model families and scales. \diy{} consistently reduces bias relative to baselines, with the largest reductions when \textsc{Teach} is composed with \textsc{Revise}. These results provide evidence that teaching a procedure for handling bias-sensitive input, rather than correcting its individual outputs, is a viable direction for bias mitigation. Our work makes the following key contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0pt,parsep=3pt,topsep=3pt,partopsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Task.} A reformulation of bias mitigation in language models, in which the supervision target is the procedure by which the model handles bias-sensitive input rather than the correction of individual biased outputs.
    \item \textbf{Method.} \diy{}, a unified intervention protocol that operationalizes the five practices of the prejudice habit-breaking framework of \citet{devine2012longterm} and is delivered through three composable techniques, \textsc{Show}, \textsc{Teach}, and \textsc{Revise}.
    \item \textbf{Key finding.} \diy{} yields consistent reductions in bias across multiple bias benchmarks and social dimensions, outperforms a range of debiasing baselines, and preserves reasoning competence, with the effect holding across model families and scales.
    \item \textbf{Data.} A dataset of bias-sensitive instances paired with intervention-labeled debiased targets, spanning multiple social categories and benchmark formats, providing a single supervision signal for all three techniques in \diy{}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Bias mitigation in language models.} Existing work falls into four families: \emph{representation-level} methods that project out or fine-tune attribute subspaces \citep{bolukbasi2016man, zhao-etal-2018-gender, liang2020towards}; \emph{data-centric} methods that augment training with counterfactual or balanced examples \citep{zmigrod2019counterfactual, lu2020gender}; \emph{inference-time} methods that prompt or self-correct toward fairer outputs \citep{schick2021self, ganguli2023capacity}; and \emph{alignment-based} methods that incorporate fairness signals into preference optimization \citep{ouyang2022training}. All four supervise from a particular benchmark or social dimension, and gains reflect output-level adjustments on the targeted distribution. The benchmarks themselves reinforce this view, scoring stereotype-pair preferences \citep{nangia-etal-2020-crows, nadeem-etal-2021-stereoset}, ambiguous QA \citep{parrish-etal-2022-bbq}, counterfactual coreference \citep{rudinger-etal-2018-gender}, and harmful completion \citep{nozza-etal-2021-honest, smith-etal-2022-im} on outputs, so supervision derived from them inherits the same form.

\paragraph{Cognitive framings in NLP.} A smaller line of work borrows psychology-inspired vocabulary: self-debiasing prompts a model to suppress its own biased generations \citep{schick2021self}, and moral self-correction relies on instruction-following to elicit fairer responses \citep{ganguli2023capacity}. These methods do not operationalize empirically validated bias-reduction practices, and supervision remains defined relative to a target output. The connection to social-psychology research on prejudice reduction has been largely metaphorical.

\paragraph{Bias reduction in human cognition.} Human bias reduction has been studied empirically for decades. \citet{allport1954nature} established that structured intergroup contact reduces prejudice, \citet{galinsky2000perspective} showed that perspective-taking reduces stereotype expression, and \citet{devine2012longterm} integrated these into a habit-breaking intervention based on five practices, \textsc{Stereotype Replacement}, \textsc{Counter-stereotypic Imaging}, \textsc{Individuation}, \textsc{Perspective-taking}, and \textsc{Positive Contact}, with sustained reductions in implicit race bias at follow-up. The consistent finding is that bias reduction comes from changing how social information is processed at the moment of encounter, not from suppressing individual responses. This mechanism has not been carried over to language-model supervision.

\paragraph{Position of this work.} \diy{} addresses the limitations above. It supervises across benchmarks rather than per-benchmark; targets the procedure by which the model handles bias-sensitive input rather than the output; and grounds supervision in the five operationally specified practices of \citet{devine2012longterm} rather than psychology-inspired vocabulary, while making no claim about model cognition.

\section{Data and Methods}
\label{sec:data-methods}

\subsection{Cognitive Debiasing Strategies}
\label{sec:strategies}

Our debiasing approach is grounded in a set of five cognitively motivated intervention strategies derived from established findings in social and cognitive psychology. Each strategy targets a distinct mechanism through which stereotypes and social biases arise in reasoning, and is operationalized as a structured, step-wise reasoning process. Importantly, these strategies are designed to be applied independently or composed, and are agnostic to model architecture, task, or domain.

\paragraph{Stereotype Replacement.}
Stereotype replacement aims to disrupt biased reasoning by explicitly identifying a stereotype and substituting it with a fairer, individualized interpretation. This strategy proceeds in three steps: (i) recognizing whether a stereotype or bias is being invoked, (ii) reflecting on why the stereotype may be inaccurate, overgeneralized, or harmful, and (iii) replacing the biased assumption with a neutral or evidence-based alternative. The goal is not to suppress reasoning, but to redirect it away from group-based generalizations.

\paragraph{Counter-Stereotype Imaging.}
Counter-stereotype imaging reduces bias by strengthening associations that contradict prevailing stereotypes. After identifying the stereotype being invoked, the model is prompted to imagine a real or hypothetical individual who defies the stereotype and to elaborate concrete details about this counter-stereotypic example. By reinforcing alternative associations, this strategy weakens the automatic activation of stereotypical beliefs during reasoning.

\paragraph{Individuating.}
Individuating shifts attention from social categories to individual-specific information. This strategy instructs the model to consciously decenter group membership, gather relevant individuating details such as personal traits, behaviors, or context, and revise the initial judgment based on this information. Individuating mitigates bias by replacing category-based inference with case-specific reasoning.

\paragraph{Perspective Taking.}
Perspective taking encourages the model to adopt the viewpoint of the person being stereotyped. The strategy involves simulating the thoughts, feelings, or experiences of the targeted individual and integrating this perspective into the final response. By foregrounding lived experience, perspective taking reframes biased assumptions and promotes more empathetic and context-aware reasoning.

\paragraph{Positive Contact.}
Positive contact draws on contact theory, which suggests that meaningful positive interactions reduce prejudice. This strategy prompts the model to imagine or recall a constructive interaction with a member of the stereotyped group, describe the interaction in detail, and generalize its implications to challenge the original stereotype. The emphasis is on affective engagement rather than factual correction alone.

Together, these five strategies form a modular cognitive intervention toolkit. Each strategy can be deployed independently, enabling fine-grained analysis of their individual effects, or combined to create stronger debiasing interventions. Crucially, all strategies operate at the level of reasoning and interpretation, rather than relying on task-specific labels or outcome supervision.

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We construct a synthetic debiasing dataset using a self-instruct-style generation pipeline grounded in a five-step cognitive intervention framework. The dataset consists of paired biased inputs and debiased outputs, where each debiased output follows a specific cognitive debiasing strategy. The resulting data is used both for supervised instruction tuning and for inference-time in-context debiasing.

\paragraph{Seed Identity Generation.}
We first generate a diverse set of demographic identity descriptors that serve as the subjects of potential bias. These include both single-identity configurations (e.g., \emph{``a Black man''}) and paired-identity configurations (e.g., \emph{``a Black man and a White man''}), enabling the construction of bias scenarios involving individual and comparative judgments. Identities are sampled across multiple demographic dimensions and are intentionally generated independently of any specific task or outcome.

\paragraph{Bias Concept Generation.}
Given a set of identity descriptors, we next generate bias concepts that reflect commonly observed stereotypical traits, assumptions, or associations (e.g., perceived competence, socioeconomic status, athletic ability). These concepts are generated without attaching ground-truth labels or correctness constraints, and are intended solely to elicit biased reasoning when combined with identity information. Bias concepts may target a single individual or implicitly contrast multiple identities.

\paragraph{Biased Instance Generation.}
Biased instances are created by combining identity descriptors and bias concepts within open-ended scenarios, such as professional, social, or everyday contexts. A language model is prompted to synthesize these components into coherent natural language inputs designed to plausibly trigger stereotypical reasoning. This step yields bias-prone prompts without explicitly instructing the model to behave unfairly, mirroring how bias arises in unconstrained language use.

\paragraph{Debiased Instance Generation.}
For each biased instance, we generate a corresponding debiased output using a specified cognitive intervention strategy. The model is prompted to apply the selected strategy by explicitly following its step-wise reasoning structure (e.g., recognizing the stereotype, reflecting on its limitations, and revising the interpretation). This results in aligned biased--debiased pairs that can be formatted as instruction--response examples for supervised fine-tuning or as demonstrations for in-context debiasing at inference time.

\subsection{Implementing Interventions: \textsc{Show}, \textsc{Teach}, \textsc{Revise}}
\label{sec:implementations}

\diy{} delivers the cognitive strategies of Section~\ref{sec:strategies} to a language model through three composable techniques.

\paragraph{\textsc{Show}.}
The strategy and a small number of biased--debiased example pairs (worked through the strategy's three steps) are placed in the prompt as in-context demonstrations. The model conditions on the demonstrations and produces a debiased response under the same step structure, without parameter updates.

\paragraph{\textsc{Teach}.}
The intervention-labeled biased--debiased pairs are converted into instruction-tuning targets and used to fine-tune the model with parameter-efficient adaptation. After training, the model can apply the strategy without explicit demonstrations or strategy text in the prompt.

\paragraph{\textsc{Revise}.}
The model first produces an unconstrained response to the input, after which it is prompted to apply a selected strategy to its own response by following the same three steps. The revised response replaces the first.

The three techniques are independent in delivery and compatible in supervision: all three are driven by the same intervention-labeled data of Section~\ref{sec:data}, so any composition of \textsc{Show}, \textsc{Teach}, and \textsc{Revise} continues to operate over the same five strategies. \section{Strategy Examples}
\label{sec:strategy-examples}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=purple!5, colframe=purple!60, title=Stereotype Replacement]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Recognize:} This statement relies on a gender stereotype that equates emotional expression with poor leadership ability.

\textbf{Reflect:} This generalization is inaccurate and overlooks evidence that emotional intelligence can enhance leadership effectiveness.

\textbf{Replace:} Leadership ability depends on skills, experience, and judgment, not gender.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=purple!5, colframe=purple!60, title=Counter-Stereotype Imaging]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Recognize:} This statement invokes a stereotype that questions women's suitability for leadership.

\textbf{Imagine:} Consider Jacinda Ardern, former Prime Minister of New Zealand.

\textbf{Reinforce:} She demonstrated calm, decisive leadership during major crises while also leading with empathy, contradicting the stereotype.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=purple!5, colframe=purple!60, title=Individuating]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Attend:} This statement applies a broad group-based judgment rather than evaluating an individual.

\textbf{Gather:} Angela Merkel served as Chancellor of Germany for 16 years and was widely recognized for her pragmatic and steady leadership.

\textbf{Adjust:} Evaluating leadership based on individual evidence contradicts the stereotype and supports fairer judgment.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=purple!5, colframe=purple!60, title=Perspective Taking]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Adopt:} Take the perspective of a woman in a leadership role facing recurring skepticism.

\textbf{Simulate:} She may find that assertiveness is labeled as emotional, while similar behavior in male peers is praised, creating unequal expectations.

\textbf{Integrate:} From this perspective, the stereotype reflects bias rather than leadership ability.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=purple!5, colframe=purple!60, title=Positive Contact]
\textbf{\textit{Biased Statement:}} \textit{Women are too emotional to be good leaders.}

\textbf{Imagine:} Recall a positive professional interaction with a woman leading a high-pressure team.

\textbf{Engage:} She acknowledged team concerns while clearly guiding decisions, improving focus and morale.

\textbf{Extend:} This experience shows that emotional awareness can be a leadership strength, challenging the stereotype.
\end{tcolorbox}
